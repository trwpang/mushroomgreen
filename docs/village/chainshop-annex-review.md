## Position and visible form

Earth shows the forge's long axis running roughly northeast–southwest beside the lane on its east/southeast side. The compact upper-storey block sits at its northern end, offset west/northwest, with a roof transverse to the main ridge. This agrees with the supplied ground photograph: looking towards the entrance gable, the taller block is on the right, behind the gable plane. Position is a visual interpretation, not surveyed coordinates.

The photograph supplies useful modelling details:

- A narrow two-storey brick block attached to the workshop, not a detached generic cottage or lean-to.
- Its own pitched tile roof, visible gable side, higher eaves and a tight roof/wall junction.
- A small upper window, lower door and visibly different brick patches between storeys.
- Mixed red, brown and dark bricks, pale mortar repairs, irregular staining and a darker wall base.
- On the main forge: substantial chimney stacks, boarded shutters, small ventilation openings and an uneven tiled roof.

The bright green door, white window finish, signs, restoration repairs and clipped shrubs describe the modern photograph. They are not evidence for those exact finishes in 1865.

## Identity and chronology

Keep three structures separate:

1. The main chainshop.
2. Its surviving small attached block, plausibly associated with the upstairs office described in the book. That identification remains an inference.
3. The book's No. 14 dwelling, said to have been demolished in 1975 and described as typical of the 1870s.

The book says there is no proof anyone lived in the attached cottage. A modern Maps pin or the listing phrase 'adjacent to Number 14' does not identify the annex as an occupied No. 14. Nor does it connect it to household 14 in our dataset. The date of the annex's present two-storey form remains unresolved.

No. 9 provides independent, much stronger evidence that early two-storey dwellings existed locally. Its listed position is west of the workshop. Match that site against our map before changing a household by number. The listing does not establish the exact date of each surviving alteration or validate an upstairs for Ambrose's No. 21.

## Implications for the village

The quoted roof-deadline story should remain oral tradition. Its speaker expresses uncertainty, and the book's rent records do not establish automatic free ownership. Do not present it as a verified enclosure rule or a proven origin of the name.

The useful visual interpretation is gradual, uneven growth: different roof heights, additions made in separate phases, mixed wall materials, shared working yards and paths fitted around existing buildings. An unplanned settlement need not consist of uniformly tiny, single-storey shacks.

Prioritise the annex silhouette and attachment in the next forge model pass. Use existing brick, tile and timber materials with local variation. Keep its domestic use and its presence in exactly 1865 labelled as interpretations until further evidence resolves them. Avoid adding a complete inhabited household or a later wage office as an established 1865 fact.
